The Universal Language of Patterns

Why do civilizations separated by thousands of miles repeatedly produce intricate geometric patterns? Why do circles, spirals, interlocking lines, nested structures, and repeating motifs appear in Tibetan monasteries, Indian temples, Islamic architecture, Persian carpets, and the visionary art of the Amazon?

And why do remarkably similar forms appear in nature—in flowers, seashells, river networks, snowflakes, and even the organization of living cells?

These questions inspired our project, Psychedelic Visions, an interactive website combining nine forms of dynamically generated art with music and sound.

The objective is not simply to produce beautiful animations. It is to explore a deeper idea: patterns are one of humanity's oldest ways of understanding reality, and mathematics gives us a language for describing how those patterns emerge, repeat, transform, and organize themselves.

1. India and Tibet: The mandala as a representation of the cosmos

The Sanskrit word mandala refers to a circle, but its significance extends beyond circular geometry. In Hindu and Buddhist traditions, mandalas can represent an ordered universe, a sacred space, or a structured path of contemplation.

Tibetan mandalas frequently organize their imagery around a central palace surrounded by concentric geometries. The Rubin Museum describes them as symbolic representations of the cosmos used in meditation and visualization.

Indian yantras, including the Sri Yantra, explore another geometric language: interlocking triangles, concentric enclosures, lotus petals, and carefully organized centers.

The significance is both mathematical and contemplative. Complex relationships become visible as ordered geometry. Attention moves from the outer structure toward the center, while individual elements acquire meaning through their relationship to the whole.

Our animated mandalas reinterpret these principles through continuously unfolding geometric constructions.

2. The Peruvian Amazon: Patterns, visions, and sound

Among the Shipibo-Konibo people of the Peruvian Amazon, the artistic tradition known as kené consists of intricate geometric designs found in textiles, ceramics, body painting, and other artistic forms.

These designs are connected to Indigenous cosmology, plant knowledge, and visionary experiences associated with ayahuasca. Peru recognized kené as national cultural heritage in 2008.

An especially fascinating aspect is the relationship between visual patterns and sound. In Shipibo-Konibo traditions, geometric designs are associated with ícaros, ceremonial songs. Anthropological accounts describe relationships between sung patterns, envisioned designs, and traditional healing practices.

This does not establish that sound and geometry have identical physical effects. Rather, it demonstrates a cultural tradition in which visual form, sound, memory, and spiritual experience are closely connected.

Our Amazonian animations explore that relationship through evolving patterns synchronized with music.

3. Islamic geometry and the Naqshbandi Sufi tradition

Islamic civilization developed extraordinarily sophisticated geometric art, visible in mosques, manuscripts, architectural surfaces, and carpets.

Stars, polygons, interlocking lines, and repeating structures create compositions that can extend across an entire surface without losing their underlying mathematical order.

There is also an interesting linguistic connection to the Naqshbandi Sufi order, named after the fourteenth-century Central Asian spiritual teacher Bahauddin Naqshband of Bukhara.

The Persian word naqsh (نقش) means pattern, and also design, image, or imprint, while band relates to binding or fastening. The name Naqshband has traditionally been interpreted as engraving the remembrance of God upon the heart through sustained spiritual practice.

The connection is therefore meaningful, but requires precision: the Persian word naqsh literally means pattern, and is embedded in the name of the Naqshbandi Sufi order. This does not establish that the order originated Islamic geometric art.

Nevertheless, the metaphor is striking. A repeated spiritual practice creates an enduring inner imprint, just as repeated geometric relationships produce a coherent visual structure.

Our Islamic geometry animations investigate symmetry, repetition, recursion, and the emergence of intricate forms from relatively simple rules.

4. Christopher Alexander: From mathematics to a pattern language

One of the most remarkable modern contributors to this discussion was Christopher Alexander (1936–2022).

Alexander studied mathematics at Cambridge University and earned his doctorate in architecture at Harvard. He later became a professor at the University of California, Berkeley.

His influential book, A Pattern Language (1977), described 253 recurring design patterns that could help create more humane buildings and communities.

Alexander's work subsequently influenced computer science, particularly the development of software design patterns.

But Alexander went much further.

In A Foreshadowing of 21st Century Art, he studied the color and geometry of early Turkish carpets. In his four-volume The Nature of Order, he developed the idea that certain structural properties recur across natural and human-made forms.

These include strong centers, levels of scale, local symmetries, boundaries, alternating repetition, and deeply interconnected structures.

His central argument was that beauty and coherence arise not simply from symmetry but from the relationships among structures at different scales.

This is particularly relevant to our animations. A pattern can begin with a simple center and develop increasingly intricate supporting forms without losing its overall organization.

Alexander's theories are influential interpretations of structure and beauty, not universally established laws of physics. Nevertheless, they offer a powerful framework for understanding why certain patterns appear coherent and alive.

5. Mathematics: The hidden structure of natural patterns

Modern mathematics allows us to go beyond recognizing patterns to investigating the mechanisms that produce them.

Spiral arrangements in plants, branching networks, crystalline symmetries, wave interference, and fractal structures can all be studied through mathematical models.

In 1952, Alan Turing proposed that interacting chemical processes could spontaneously generate biological patterns through reaction and diffusion.

His mathematical framework helps explain how ordered structures such as stripes, spots, and labyrinth-like arrangements can emerge without an external designer specifying each detail.

Fractal mathematics provides another insight: relatively simple rules, repeated recursively, can generate structures of extraordinary complexity.

The significance is profound. Complexity does not necessarily require complicated instructions. Sometimes it emerges from simple relationships operating repeatedly across different scales.

This is why mathematical animations are particularly useful. They make the process of pattern formation visible, rather than showing only the finished pattern.

5A. Patterns, human knowledge, and the rise of artificial intelligence

Patterns are central not only to art and mathematics, but to human knowledge itself. Information becomes useful knowledge when we recognize relationships within it: similarities, differences, regularities, sequences, causes, and recurring structures. In this sense, much of knowledge can be understood as patterned information—information organized so that we can interpret the past, explain the present, and anticipate what may happen next.

A child learns to recognize faces, voices, and language by discovering regularities in experience. Scientists search for patterns in experimental observations. Physicians interpret patterns of symptoms and measurements. Engineers identify relationships among forces, materials, and structural behavior. Mathematics gives many of these relationships precise form, allowing us to test them, generalize them, and use them to make predictions.

The history of artificial intelligence provides a striking modern example. Early AI research pursued two broad approaches: systems that represented intelligence through explicit rules and symbolic reasoning, and systems that learned patterns from examples through artificial neural networks. Rule-based expert systems proved valuable in constrained domains, but hand-crafting rules for the enormous variability of natural images, speech, and other sensory information was exceptionally difficult.

Modern machine learning—and especially deep learning—transformed those fields by allowing multilayer neural networks to learn representations and statistical patterns from data rather than requiring every feature and decision rule to be specified in advance. Breakthroughs in speech recognition and computer vision helped establish this approach, which now underpins much of contemporary AI, including language models. Deep learning still uses algorithms and mathematical rules; the crucial change is that many of the useful representations are learned instead of manually encoded.

This connects the ancient and modern strands of our project. A mandala, a Persian carpet, an Amazonian design, a natural branching system, and a neural network are not the same phenomenon. Yet each invites us to investigate how relationships among elements create structure, how structure carries meaning, and how complex forms can emerge from simpler organizing principles.

To recognize patterns is to begin to understand; to discover the rules and relationships that generate them is to deepen that understanding. Our evolving animations make this idea visible, while music adds the dimension of patterned information unfolding through time.

6. Why combine these patterns with music?

Music itself is mathematical. Rhythm organizes events in time, pitch is related to frequency, and harmony depends on relationships among oscillations.

Visual geometry organizes relationships in space. Music organizes relationships in time.

Combining them creates an opportunity to explore how the human brain responds to structured sensory information.

Research supports the potential benefits of music for stress reduction and anxiety. A 2025 meta-analysis of 51 trials found a moderate benefit of music therapy for self-reported anxiety, although physiological outcomes were less conclusive.

A separate review of 25 soundscape experiments, including research published in English and Chinese, found promising benefits from combining auditory and visual experiences, while cautioning that many studies had methodological limitations.

This is why our website incorporates sound alongside the animations. The intention is to create an interactive environment for aesthetic exploration, concentration, and contemplation—not to claim that particular frequencies or patterns have proven healing powers.

7. What do these traditions have in common?

The traditions are culturally distinct and should not be reduced to a single spiritual system. Nevertheless, several structural ideas recur.

The shared lesson is not that all civilizations discovered the same hidden equation. It is that humans repeatedly use ordered relationships to interpret the natural world, construct meaningful environments, and organize experience.

8. Psychedelic Visions: Bringing ancient pattern traditions into the digital world

Our project brings these traditions together through modern generative computing.

Unlike a painting, carpet, or static mandala, a computer-generated pattern can continuously evolve. Its geometry can grow, subdivide, rotate, and transform. Its colors can respond to sound. Its structure can become more complex without repeating the same image.

The computer makes it possible to explore the process of pattern formation.

And this leads to the central philosophical idea behind the project:

Patterns are not merely decorations placed upon reality. They are often expressions of the relationships through which physical, biological, and mathematical systems organize themselves.

Mathematics does not prove that every traditional sacred pattern describes a physical law. But it does provide a common language for investigating symmetry, repetition, proportion, recursion, and emergence.

Psychedelic Visions is therefore both an artistic experiment and an educational exploration. It connects cultural traditions, mathematics, computer graphics, music, and human perception.

It invites visitors not simply to look at patterns, but to watch them emerge—and to experience the relationships among geometry, sound, and time.
